## Setting Overview [CONFIRMED]

**Corvid Cove** — final name, locked in [CONFIRMED] — is a small Pacific Northwest coastal
town, population small enough that everyone recognizes everyone. It sits on a peninsula:
evergreen mountains at its back, water on three sides. One two-lane road connects it to the
outside world, winding through a mountain pass — still called **Sable Pass**, a holdover from
the town's original name — that washes out or closes for rockslides most winters. A small ferry
dock handles the rest of the outside traffic when the road's bad. When the weather turns, Corvid
Cove is functionally an island.

This isolation is load-bearing, not decoration — it's the reason the Pact has to handle things
themselves instead of calling in outside help, and the reason strange things can happen here
for decades without anyone from outside noticing or caring.

**The town has had two names.** It was **Sable Cove** for roughly ninety years — from Josiah
Sable's 1817 founding straight through the resettlement and the massacre — before being renamed
**Corvid Cove** in the early 1900s (see *Founding & Layered History*, below). The old name never
fully left: it survives in Sable Pass, in Sable Chapel, in Founders Hollow. Corvid Cove has had
residents on this stretch of coast for more than two centuries, across two separate settlements
over thirty years apart, and every era — under either name — has left something behind.

**Recent tourist boom [CONFIRMED]:** in the last several years, Corvid Cove has grown fast and
seasonally — summers especially, with a smaller winter bump — as visitors discover the coastline
and money follows them. New restaurants, rentals, guided hikes, whale-watching charters, that
kind of thing. Year-round population is still small and everyone-knows-everyone; tourist season
swells it noticeably, brings in seasonal workers chasing the sudden demand for labor, and gives
the town a strange split personality — sleepy and closed-in most of the year, crowded and
performing "quaint coastal town" for a few months at a stretch. This is also the practical reason
new PCs can plausibly be in Corvid Cove at all — see *Who You Are*, below.

### Who You Are — PC Concept Buckets [CONFIRMED]

Three broad buckets for open-table character concepts, useful for getting a new player playable
in minutes:

- **Local** — grew up here, or close enough. Knows people, knows the town's unspoken rules, has
  history here that hunts can hook into.
- **Transplant** — moved here recently for the tourism-economy work (a restaurant, a charter
  outfit, a rental management company, seasonal guiding). Still learning the town's rhythms;
  doesn't yet know what everyone's carefully not mentioning.
- **Stranded tourist** — came for a visit, ended up staying — car trouble, a job offer they
  didn't expect to take, a reason to avoid going home, or something about the town that won't
  quite let them leave. Outsider's eye, weakest local ties, easiest to drop a new player into
  cold.

## "IT" — Why Nobody Else Seems to Care [CONFIRMED]

Something about Corvid Cove makes its people not want to look too closely. Whether it's a
genuine supernatural effect, generations of learned small-town avoidance, or some blend of
both is a season-long mystery, not a rule to be explained upfront — **keep it deliberately
unnamed in-fiction.** Characters refer to it as "it," never with a proper name; NPCs who start to name it tend to change the subject, physically flinch, or just trail off. The Pact can spend the whole campaign chasing the question of what "IT" actually is.

**Mechanically, this is a Keeper tool, not a hard rule:**
- Background NPCs downplay, rationalize, and forget strange events faster than they realistically should. A missing person becomes "oh, they moved away, I think." A mauled livestock becomes "cougars again." Something the Pact clearly witnessed becomes, within days, a story nobody but them remembers happening.
- A few NPCs resist this pull — not immune, just stubborn or already too deep in it to stop looking (Imogen Pryce at the library is the clearest example; Eugene Marsh and Warrick Sol both qualify too, in their own ways). These are the Pact's natural allies and a *very* effective way to inject a Void Clue.
- Reflavor town-wide Alarm/Panic (BID p. 67) as running unnaturally low by default — the town's baseline disposition is denial, which makes the Pact's job harder because nobody else will corroborate what they're seeing.
- Play this up gently in early sessions and let players start connecting it themselves. It should feel uncanny before it's ever confirmed as supernatural.

**Dormant vs. hungry — the loose working model [holding pattern, not locked]:** most of the
time, "IT" is asleep or something close to it. Even asleep, it "leaks" into its surroundings —
this is the in-fiction explanation for most weekly monster-of-the-week hunts. Strange creatures,
localized bad weather, animals acting wrong, people acting wrong (Crane's spree may itself have
been a leak expressing through a person rather than an obviously supernatural event — deliberately
left ambiguous whether he acted purely on his own). It doesn't need to be touched to affect
things: it can act on people and animals and weather at a distance, and it's genuinely good at
making people see, hear, or believe things that aren't there — hallucination and manipulation are
default tools, not an escalation. A direct physical manifestation should be rare and mean
something — the entity spending real accumulated power, not a default state.

On rarer occasions it wakes and gets hungry — for what, exactly, is unknown even to the Keeper
right now (fear, pain, suffering, blood, all guesses). These waking periods are good material for
bigger, more directed arc-level happenings rather than one-off hunts. **Whether the debt Sable
never paid is still outstanding, and whether the entity's hunger cycles are its way of collecting
against it, is an open thread worth letting players theorize about rather than confirming.** One
live possibility: it eventually "wakes" Sable himself, or what's left of him, to finally settle
what he owes — not committed to canon, just sitting there as an option.

**On purpose, none of this is fully defined.** Bump in the Dark is built around the Keeper not
knowing the full shape of the threat until the players help find it through clues and their own
theories — that collaborative discovery is the point, not a placeholder for missing prep. Update
this section as answers emerge in actual play rather than deciding them in advance.

## Time & Isolation [CONFIRMED]

**Modern day**, but comms are unreliable by design:
- **Cell service** comes from a single tower up on the pass. Storms, fog, and "equipment
  trouble" take it down often enough that nobody's surprised anymore. Coverage inside town is
  spotty at best; it's dead the moment you're in the trees or on the water.
- **The only reliable internet and landline connection** in town runs through the **Cormorant
  Historical Society & Public Library** (POI #5, below) — it has the town's one real uplink,
  installed for the county records digitization grant a few years back. If the Pact needs to
  actually look something up online, or call someone off-peninsula who'll pick up, this is where they go.
- **During a hunt**, comms conveniently die — weather, a blown transformer, "the tower's down
  again." Keepers should use this as a framing tool, not a hard rule: isolate the Pact when the scene calls for it. Over time, players may start to notice comms die *specifically* around void activity — that's intentional, and can pay off later as a Void Clue in its own right (see *"IT,"* above).

## Tone & Aesthetic [CONFIRMED]

**Primary touchstones:** *Widow's Bay* · *Twin Peaks* (original run) · *Goosebumps* · *Supernatural*
(monster-of-the-week seasons)

**Mood:** Small-town-familiar tipping into wrong. Everybody knows everybody, which makes it
worse when someone starts acting like they don't know themselves. Goosebumps-style hook and
escalation (something's off → it gets worse → confront it → uneasy resolution) delivered with
Supernatural's monster-of-the-week structure and Twin Peaks' sense that the town itself is
half the mystery. Horror should have real teeth — consequences land — but each episode resolves enough to send everyone home. Save the unresolved dread for the season-level Void Clock.

**Episodic anthology structure [CONFIRMED]:** matches the user's open-table format directly —
every session is a self-contained "episode," playable by whoever shows up that week, while the Void Clock quietly accumulates in the background across the season toward a Finale. No session should require a specific player to be present to resolve.

## Design Philosophy [CONFIRMED]

- **Low-prep, high-reuse.** Bump in the Dark is a light system by design — lean into that. Build
  a small, reusable cast (the diner waitress, the librarian, the bartender) and a handful of
  strong locations rather than a sprawling setting bible. New NPCs and locations get added
  organically as sessions demand them, not pre-built in bulk.
- **The town is a character.** Corvid Cove's isolation, history, and small-town social fabric
  should show up in every hunt — who'd notice something missing, who'd cover it up, who's been quietly dealing with this for decades.
- **Everyone's a local.** Default Pact concept: the hunters are Corvid Cove residents (or
  recent transplants with a real reason to stay), not outside monster-hunters passing through. Their stake in the town is personal.
- **History is layered, not upfront.** Every POI below carries real history — some of it
  common local knowledge, some of it buried. None of it should be dumped on players in one go; it surfaces in pieces, across sessions, as clues and conversations warrant. The throughline
  (see below) is meant to be *assembled* by the Pact over a season, not handed to them.

## Founding & Layered History [CONFIRMED]

A single stretch of coastline — the cliffs at what's now called **Cutter's Bluff** — has quietly accumulated tragedy across five different eras. No one in town currently connects them all, and the town's own official history of the earliest one is a lie. That connection, and that lie, are the campaign's slow-burn throughline.

### Before 1817 — The Kellewa

The **Kellewa** — an invented people, native to this stretch of coast for generations before
Sable's arrival — knew the cove and the bluff above it long before any settler did, and
deliberately did not build directly on that ground. Not out of taboo alone: their oral tradition held that something old lived in the water and rock there, that it noticed people, and that it was best left unnoticed in turn. When Sable's party arrived and started building anyway, Kellewa elders warned them off more than once. The warnings were not heeded, and in journal entries from the time may have been interpreted as threats. 

**This detail matters mechanically:** the Kellewa are not the source of the danger, and their
absence from the immediate cove is *why* Sable's party found it "empty" and unclaimed. They are, if anything, the only people in this story who ever understood the danger correctly.

### 1817–19 — The First Settlement, and the Deal

Maritime and overland fur traders had been working this stretch of coast since the late 1700s —
by the 1810s, a handful of trading posts already dotted the region. **Josiah Sable**, a former
fur trapper who split off from a larger coastal trading outfit rather than move on with it, led
a small party — a few families, not a colony — to settle the cove and gave the town its name.
This was not one of the large agricultural waves that would arrive a generation later; just a
handful of people staking a claim on a hard, remote piece of coastline. Sable built the
settlement's one stone building by hand that first year — a modest one-room chapel, his name and
the founding year cut into the cornerstone. **Sable Chapel** still stands today.

The settlement's second winter (1818–19) turned brutal fast — worse than anything the Kellewa
had warned them a normal winter could be. Sable's colony was starving, freezing, and dying by
inches when Josiah Sable, whether he understood what he was doing or not, **made a deal with
whatever lived at the bluff** to see his people through it. The book's own "deal with the
darkness" mechanic (BID p. 14) is the mechanical shorthand for exactly this kind of moment — a
bonus against impossible odds, for a listed cost. Sable took the bonus. **What the cost actually
was, and whether he understood it going in, is deliberately left unrecorded** [TBD — should stay
a mystery uncovered mid-to-late campaign, not exposition].

He didn't pay it. Whatever he owed came due, and **the entire settlement vanished** — no bodies,
no signs of struggle, no evidence of a mass exodus by land or sea, cabins found later with meals
half-eaten and fires long cold. Sable Chapel was the only structure left standing. Whether this
was punishment, collection, or something colder is not something anyone currently in Corvid Cove
knows.

The land sat abandoned for roughly three decades.

### The Official Record — and the Lie Underneath It

The story that survived, the one written into every local history and repeated without question
for two hundred years, is that **the Kellewa massacred Sable's colony.** It is not true. It was
never true. Nobody currently alive in town knows that it isn't.

Where the lie came from is easy to guess and worse to sit with: trappers and traders passing
through the abandoned settlement in the years after found a story that made sense to them, that
didn't require believing anything impossible, and that fit what they already assumed about the
land's original people. The lie outlived everyone who might have corrected it.

### 1852 — Resettlement

A new wave of settlers arrived — part of the broader push of coastal Washington and Oregon
settlement in this period — found the overgrown remains of the original colony and the intact
chapel, and rebuilt on the same site. Some considered the land cursed and argued for a fresh
name; the majority kept "Sable Cove," partly out of respect, partly because the chapel's carved
cornerstone made the original name feel already decided. They also brought the story with them —
that the tribe up the coast had wiped out the first colony. Sable Chapel still stands today,
attached to the cemetery (POI #3, below) — roof patched more than once, rarely used for services,
but never torn down.

### Mid-1850s — The Massacre

Regional tension between settlers and Pacific Northwest tribes was already high through this
period. In Sable Cove, the decades-old story of "what happened to the first colony" became the
justification a local settler militia used to attack the Kellewa directly — framed at the time,
and recorded ever since, as retaliation for a killing that never actually happened. Most of the
Kellewa in the immediate area were killed; survivors scattered, some absorbed into other coastal
communities, a small number of descendants still traceable today (see *Beyond the Peninsula*,
below). The town has never corrected the record. Its own founding myth is built on a massacre
it still tells itself was justified.

**How this interacts with "IT":** the archive gap and the town's selective memory (see *"IT,"*
above) may not need any supernatural help to keep this quiet — small towns are frequently very
good at forgetting their own atrocities without any outside force required. Keepers can leave it
ambiguous how much of the silence is "IT" and how much is ordinary human shame. That ambiguity is
the point.

### 1903 — The Rename

Theodore Roosevelt's real 1903 tour of the Pacific coast is the historical peg this hangs on — as
local legend tells it (and local legend is doing a lot of work here), Roosevelt made a stop at
Sable Cove specifically to see its unusually large, unusually bold local population of
crows — a distinctive coastal offshoot of the Northwestern Crow, thick enough in number and
strange enough in behavior that word of them had reached him. Whatever actually happened during
that visit, the town seized the moment: within a year or two it had rebranded itself **Corvid
Cove**, trading a founder's surname for a marketable, presidentially-adjacent bit of civic
identity.

The old name didn't disappear so much as get demoted — Sable Pass, Sable Chapel, and Founders
Hollow all kept their names, and the town's oldest deed records and gravestones still say Sable
Cove. Nobody currently in town thinks of this as sinister. It's a fun piece of local trivia,
proudly repeated to tourists, entirely separate in most people's minds from anything darker. It
also happens to be the exact kind of quirky historical footnote a small town produces on its own,
with no supernatural help required — much like the massacre, above.

**A texture note, not a rule:** the crows themselves are worth leaning into as ambient atmosphere.
They gather in unusual numbers around Founders Hollow Cemetery and Cutter's Bluff specifically,
and old-timers have a loose, half-joking sense that the birds "know" when something's wrong
before people do. Keepers can use a sudden mass of crows going silent, or gathering somewhere
they shouldn't, as a free, non-verbal tension cue — it costs nothing narratively and it's already
baked into the town's own self-image.

### 1918–1919 — The Grey Fever

Filed at the time under the same catch-all as everywhere else in the country — "Spanish
influenza" — and folded quietly into the national death toll without much scrutiny. It wasn't
that. Whatever actually moved through Corvid Cove that winter turned the skin an ashen grey
before anything else showed, put its victims into a shuffling, feverish half-sleep that had them
wandering out of their homes and into the streets and woods at all hours, and in its worst stage
made them violent — clawing, biting, lashing out at anyone close enough, especially after dark.
Not quite anything with a name Keepers should reach for outright — closer to a fever-dream than
a monster movie — but it's very hard for a modern table not to think the word anyway, and that's
fine. Let them.

The cemetery couldn't keep pace; several **mass graves** were dug fast and shallow at the back
of the grounds, some poorly recorded, a few not recorded at all. Superstition took root fast —
that the sickness had "come up out of the old ground," that the original settlement's oldest
plots shouldn't have been disturbed. Formal upkeep declined sharply after this and never really
recovered.

### 1943 — The *Petrel*

During the war, a small Coast Guard patrol vessel, the **USCGC *Petrel***, ran coastal watch
duty along this stretch of shoreline. It went down in a sudden, savage storm just off the
bluff — not enemy action, just weather that turned faster than anyone expected. All twelve
hands were lost; most bodies were never recovered. Years later, a local fisherman dredged up
part of the wreck — including the vessel's actual anchor — and it eventually found its way to
the bar that would be built around it and named for it: **The Drowned Anchor** (POI #6).

### Late 1970s / Early 1980s — The Cutter

**Wendell Aldous Crane**, born and raised in Corvid Cove, worked the cannery floor gutting fish —
which is where his eventual nickname started, well before anyone knew what else he was doing
with a blade. Over several years he abducted tourists and hikers passing through from the wider
Olympic Peninsula area, brought them to a secluded stretch of what was then still called
**Sentinel Point**, and killed them slowly before dumping the remains into the sea from the
cliff. When searchers and police finally closed in on him at the point itself, he went over the
edge — jumped, fell, or was pushed, depending who's telling it — and was never recovered. Presumed
dead. Locals stopped calling it Sentinel Point almost immediately after. It's been **Cutter's
Bluff** ever since (POI #2).

### The Pattern Nobody's Drawn

1819 disappearance → mid-1850s massacre → 1918-19 Grey Fever → 1943 shipwreck → 1980-ish serial
killer, all touching the same short stretch of coastline, spread across roughly 160 years, and
no one currently alive in Corvid Cove has ever laid all five next to each other. That's
deliberate — see *"IT,"* above. Imogen Pryce's private archive (POI #5) is the closest anything
in town comes to doing it, and she hasn't finished the job herself. Notably, she's found the
supernatural pattern before she's found the massacre — the town's cover-up of its own crime has
outperformed even "IT" at staying buried.

## Corvid Cove — Points of Interest [CONFIRMED]

Six anchor locations, matching the user's original list. Each is meant to be usable in any
given episode — a scene, a clue source, a place to regroup — not a one-off. Historical detail
under each entry is meant to surface gradually through play, not as an opening info-dump.

### 1. Harbor Square

The town's social center: a gazebo/bandstand, a war-and-fishing-disaster memorial with more
names on it than the town's current population would suggest, and a community notice board
that's part lost-pet flyers, part town gossip, part — if you look close and the timing's
right — a quiet record of who's gone missing over the years. Weekly farmers market draws
almost everyone in town at least once.

**Discoverable history:** the memorial's oldest section has names with no death year listed at
all — the 1819 disappearance was never formally resolved, so nobody knew what date to carve.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Dot Ferriday* — runs the farmers market, knows every family's business
three generations back, will absolutely tell you if you ask the right way (and several things
you didn't ask).

**Hook seeds:**
- A flyer on the board is for someone nobody quite remembers meeting, but everyone insists they knew.
- The memorial gains a name overnight that no one recalls losing.
- The market's quietest vendor stops showing up — nobody checks on her for two weeks, and that's the story right there.

### 2. Cutter's Bluff

A cliffside trail and beach below it — Corvid Cove's popular hike/beach spot, tide pools at low
tide, a decommissioned lighthouse at the point that the town never got around to demolishing.
Good views, cold water, and a local habit of not swimming past the rocks after dark that nobody
explains to newcomers until they ask why. Has a real day/night split: by day it's a tourist
viewpoint and a path down to a rocky beach, camera-ready and crowded in season; by night it's
where local teenagers go to hang out and make out in parked cars, which is its own kind of
exposure — kids out at the one place in town with the worst history, unsupervised, after dark.

**Discoverable history:** originally called Sentinel Point. Renamed by common local usage
(never official) after Wendell Aldous Crane — "the Cutter" — used this stretch to dispose of
his victims in the late '70s/early '80s before going over the edge himself, presumed dead, body
never recovered. It's also, unrelated on paper, the site of the 1943 sinking of the *Petrel*
(see POI #6). Two separate tragedies, forty years apart, same water. Nobody in town currently
connects them.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Warrick Sol* — beachcomber, lives out of a converted van in the bluff
parking lot most of the year, knows the tide tables better than anyone and has opinions about
what washes up that the Pact should probably start taking seriously.

**Hook seeds:**
- Something washes ashore that shouldn't exist, tagged with a decades-old maritime marking.
- A hiker goes missing on a trail with nowhere to fall and nowhere to hide.
- Lights out on the water, in a pattern, on nights when no boats are scheduled.
- A local old enough to remember lets slip that "the Cutter" was never actually found — was he?
- A carload of teenagers up here after dark sees, hears, or does something none of them can agree on afterward.

### 3. Founders Hollow Cemetery

Untended for the better part of fifty years — the last official groundskeeper retired and the
town never replaced him, so it's ivy, leaning headstones, and a gate whose padlock rusted open
years ago. **Sable Chapel** — the original 1817 stone chapel, the town's oldest surviving
structure — still stands at the back of the grounds, roof patched, rarely used. Most families
stopped visiting when the paths got too overgrown to push a wheelchair or a stroller through.
One plot near the tree line gets tended anyway, by someone nobody's ever caught in the act.

**Discoverable history:** three visibly distinct eras of graves — a handful of weathered,
nearly illegible stones from the original 1817 settlement (a few names lost entirely to
weathering); the bulk of the "normal" 19th/20th-century plots from the resettled town; and, at
the back, a cluster of unmarked or barely-marked mounds from the Grey Fever mass burials
(1918–19), which is also roughly where the neglect problem started before it got total after
the last groundskeeper quit.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Eugene Marsh* — the retired groundskeeper, still alive, still local,
insists he didn't quit so much as was quietly told to stop coming. Won't say by whom unless
pushed hard.

**Hook seeds:**
- A headstone's name has been filed off, deliberately, and recently.
- Fresh disturbed earth at a grave that predates anyone currently in town.
- The one tended plot belongs to no one on record — no burial permit, no death certificate, nothing.
- Sable Chapel's cornerstone has Josiah Sable's name and the year 1817 — and nothing else. No death date for him anywhere in town.

### 4. Tide To Table Diner

The Pact's natural home base — red vinyl booths, a bell over the door, coffee that's been on
the burner too long and nobody minds. Open early, open late, open through most of what Corvid
Cove considers a crisis. The back booth is unofficially reserved for whoever's dealing with
something.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Marlene Okafor* — owner and the town's real information broker; she
hears everything that gets said over her counter and remembers all of it. Feeds the Pact for
free more often than she should, because she's been quietly worried about this town for longer
than she'll admit.

**Hook seeds:**
- A regular stops ordering their usual, then stops being a regular at all.
- Marlene mentions — offhand, like it's nothing — that she's seen this before, decades ago.
- The diner's the last place in town with power during an episode-level blackout, and everyone ends up there.

### 5. Cormorant Historical Society & Public Library

Housed in the same converted building — a combined small-town library and local history
archive, newspaper clippings back to the town's founding, microfiche nobody but the librarian
knows how to use properly, and the town's one reliable internet connection (see *Time &
Isolation*, above). This is where research actually happens — and where the four eras of
*Founding & Layered History* can actually be pieced together, if anyone bothers to look.

**Discoverable history:** the archive has a genuine, unexplained gap — nearly all local records
from the winter of 1818–19 are simply missing, not damaged, missing. The mid-1850s coverage of
"the attack on the first colony" is thorough, confident, and — though nobody researching it
would know this yet — entirely wrong. Grey Fever-era (1918–19) coverage is folded almost
entirely into national Spanish-flu reporting — technically accurate, deliberately incomplete,
and missing every detail that made it not just the flu. Coverage of the
Crane case (late '70s/early '80s) is thorough right up until his disappearance over the bluff,
then thins out fast, like the town wanted to stop talking about it.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Imogen Pryce* — town librarian and unofficial archivist, has spent
years quietly cataloguing Corvid Cove's stranger incidents into a filing system she's never
shown anyone. She's the only person in town who's noticed the four eras might be connected —
she just hasn't said it out loud to anyone who'd believe her. She's been waiting for people who
would.

**Hook seeds:**
- The archive has a gap — a specific year or decade with every local paper missing, deliberately pulled.
- A name from the day's clues turns up in a fifty-year-old article about an eerily similar incident.
- Imogen's private files reference a pattern the Pact hasn't noticed yet, and she has, and she's scared.

### 6. The Drowned Anchor

Corvid Cove's dive bar, harborside, fishermen's territory. Low light, low ceiling, a jukebox
that only half-works, and a running catalogue of tall tales about what's out past the buoy
line — stories everyone's heard a version of, that everyone still half-believes even while
laughing them off. A corroded ship's anchor is mounted over the bar — the bar's namesake and
literal centerpiece.

**Discoverable history:** the anchor is real wreckage from the **USCGC *Petrel***, the Coast
Guard patrol boat lost with all twelve hands in a 1943 storm just off Cutter's Bluff — dredged
up years later by a local fisherman and eventually mounted here. A small brass plaque lists the
twelve names, tarnished enough that most patrons have stopped reading it.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Silas Bricke* — commercial fisherman, tells the loudest tall tales, and
is also the only person in town who's quietly stopped taking his boat out past a certain
point — roughly where the *Petrel* went down — which he never explains and nobody's pressed him
on.

**Hook seeds:**
- A fisherman comes back from a run one person short and can't — or won't — say what happened.
- A boat everyone thought was lost years ago turns up anchored in the harbor at low tide, empty.
- The tall tales start lining up with each other in ways they never used to.
- Someone points out, almost as a joke, that the *Petrel*'s twelve lost sailors were never found — same as Crane, forty years later, same stretch of water.

## The Wider Coastline — Outdoor Locations [CONFIRMED]

Beyond the six anchor POIs and Cutter's Bluff: lighter-weight outdoor locations, useful as scene
backdrops and hunt settings without needing the full NPC/hook treatment above. Add more as
sessions demand them.

**Alder Hollow** — a gentle, well-marked day-hike trail through dense mixed forest, moss-heavy
even at midday, the easy "nature walk" the tourist brochures point to. Locals mostly stick to the
outer loop. It's a small enough trail system that getting turned around on it shouldn't really be
possible — and yet, often enough to be a running local joke, people do.

**Greywood Trail** — steeper, longer, less maintained, popular with serious hikers rather than
tourists. Heavy fog and lichen-draped old growth further up; it climbs into the foothills below
Old Baldy and the two trails are sometimes confused for each other by out-of-towners, which has
caused real search-and-rescue headaches.

**Old Baldy** — the mountain at the town's back, a strenuous day-hike to a bald, treeless summit
with a sweeping view of the whole peninsula and coastline. The postcard image of the town. Also
the most direct route toward the cave system, below, for anyone who goes looking.

**Halfmoon Beach** — a second beach, gentler than Cutter's Bluff: a shallow crescent cove, real
sand instead of rock, good for swimming and tidepooling, a small strip of seasonal rentals
nearby. The family-friendly, brochure-safe counterpart to the bluff's cliffside danger.

**The caves [hidden — not common knowledge, possibly not known at all]** — somewhere in the
mountains behind town, likely accessible from Old Baldy or the higher reaches of Greywood Trail,
a cave system runs deep into the rock. Not marked on any map, not mentioned in the archive, not
something the Pact should stumble into early. Worth remembering: the Kellewa's oral tradition
(see *Founding & Layered History*) described something old living "in the water and rock" of this

## Beyond the Peninsula [CONFIRMED]

Not everything the Pact needs is in Corvid Cove — deliberate, given how much of the town's own
record is either missing or wrong.

**Notable NPC seed:** *Marguerite Colvin* — a Kellewa descendant, based off-peninsula, connected
to a small "Remembering the Kellewa" cultural/history effort (a modest center, or a seat at a
larger regional inter-tribal cultural office covering several dispersed peoples — exact shape
TBD, can emerge organically). Holds family oral history that directly contradicts Corvid Cove's
official version of the 1818–19 disappearance, but has no particular reason to trust anyone from
that town with it. Part of her ongoing work is actively correcting the record — on the record,
the Kellewa did not do this — against a town that has never bothered to ask. Reaching her at all
takes effort, given the comms/travel isolation established above; earning anything real from her
takes more. She is not a lore-dispenser — she's a person whose family history has been lied about
by this town for two hundred years, and the Pact should have to actually earn what she's willing
to share.

**Hook seeds:**
- Something the Pact finds — an object, a phrase, a symbol — turns out to be recognizable to Marguerite, and clearly upsetting.
- She already knows what really happened in 1818–19, or something close to it, passed down; she's just never had a reason to tell a Corvid Cove local before.
- She's heard of "IT" — not by that word, but she knows the old warning her ancestors gave. She won't call it a name either.

## Season One — Episode Menu & The Town Ledger [CONFIRMED, draft]

### Opening Hunt Menu

Three options to offer players at the start of the campaign (or any early open-table session) —
pick one to run, the other two roll forward as live threads on the Town Ledger, below.

1. **Something's taking the pets.** Local pets have been going missing — quietly at first, then
   enough that it's a pattern. No bodies, no mess, just empty collars and open gates. (User's
   original seed.)
2. **The tide brought something back.** After an unusual stretch of high tides and heavy fog,
   things start washing up at Cutter's Bluff that shouldn't be there — and at least one person
   who's touched what came ashore starts acting like something followed them home.
3. **Something's wrong with the [seasonal town event].** Corvid Cove has a recurring community
   event (harvest festival, founding-days celebration, whatever fits — TBD exact name/timing) and
   this year the preparations keep going wrong: decorations rearranged into shapes nobody made,
   an effigy nobody built, market food that tastes fine to most people and wrong to a few. Ties
   naturally to Harbor Square and Dot Ferriday.

### The Town Ledger & End-of-Session Roll [CONFIRMED, draft]

Keep a running list of unresolved threads the Pact hasn't fully closed out — the two unused
opening-menu options, loose ends from finished hunts, rumors overheard and never chased. This is
the Town Ledger.

**At the end of every session, roll 1d6:**

| Roll | Result |
|------|--------|
| 1–2 | **New thread.** A fresh strange occurrence enters the Ledger (pull from the Oracles, BID pp. 141–152, or improvise). |
| 3–4 | **Escalation.** Pick the oldest thread on the Ledger the Pact hasn't touched and make it worse — tick a clock, raise the stakes, let it cause a visible problem. |
| 5 | **Both.** New thread and an escalation. |
| 6 | **Quiet.** Nothing new surfaces — but tick the Void Clock (BID p. 100) quietly regardless. Even calm sessions feed the season-level dread. |

This does double duty: it keeps the open-table format stocked with ready-to-run hooks for
whoever shows up next, and it makes ignoring a thread a real cost without punishing any specific
player for missing a session.
